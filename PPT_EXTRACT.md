**Speaker notes:** SAY: Here is the problem in plain terms. Drug-target AI models often show a heat map over the protein and say "look, it found the binding site." But there are four gaps. First, that map comes from one training run, and nobody checks whether a second run gives the same map. Second, the hit rate is rarely compared with what random guessing would score. Third, the map is really a big multi-layer tensor, and the papers don't say how they reduced it to one number per residue. Fourth, the standard benchmark leaks. In DAVIS, 54 mutant proteins carry exactly the normal protein's sequence. So 12 of the 88 "unseen" test proteins in the unseen-target split are actually seen in training. IF ASKED "The DAVIS problem is already known.": Yes, and we cite it: DAVIS-complete (2025) and Ong et al. (2023). What we add is the size of the effect. We retrained DeepDTA, at the cold-target split only, without the leak and at the same training size. On the leaked rows the sequence match inflates AUROC by 0.116 (p = 0.0013, three seeds). On the strictly unleaked rows no inflation is detectable (minus 0.019, p = 0.31). We do not headline the all-rows figure of 0.019, because its interval, minus 0.022 to plus 0.059, includes zero.

**Speaker notes:** SAY: Our novelty is methodological. We did not build a better model. Other groups have already compared attention maps with binding sites: MONN, ICAN, InteractBind. What nobody had checked, as far as we found, is whether that verdict survives if you simply retrain with a different random seed. We measured this directly. Same code, same data, same settings, only the seed changes, and the verdict often flips. We also built a test battery that says when a verdict can be trusted, and we release the code so anyone can apply it. IF ASKED "Is this proven?": It is measured, not a theorem. To show that seed choice CAN flip a verdict, you need real cases where it happens, and we have 11. More seeds can tell us how often, but they cannot make those flips disappear. IF ASKED "ISAAC already reported seed stability.": ISAAC measures a different thing, intervention-based reasoning scores. Its scores were stable; our localisation verdicts are not. The two are complementary, and we say so in Discussion 4.2.

**Speaker notes:** SAY: Here is the core evidence. We trained each model three times, changing only the seed. Then we asked each run the question a normal paper asks: does this map hit binding sites better than chance? In 11 out of 22 cases, the three runs don't agree: they fall on different sides of the usual 0.05 threshold. Some say yes, some say no. Look at HyperAttentionDTI here. Same recipe, and one run is about four times better than another. Two runs would let you publish a "significant" result, and the third would not. Only 1 cell out of 22 had all three seeds pass. 10 had none pass. The spread between seeds is bigger than the distance from chance in 21 of 22 cells, and when we test seed variance directly, with Friedman and permutation tests, 7 to 8 of 22 cells show significant differences between seeds even after Holm correction. IF ASKED "Is this just GPU hardware randomness?": Possibly in part, and we do not separate the two. A seed changes the starting weights and the batch order, and GPU non-determinism adds noise on top. But the practical point holds either way: a reader who reruns the published recipe can get the opposite verdict. That is exactly what a single-run figure hides. IF ASKED "Why an uncorrected test?": On purpose. It is what a single-seed paper would report. The strict corrected test is separate and harsher: only 1 of 20 DAVIS cells survives, using the median p over the three seeds. Tested seed by seed, 5 seed-runs survive. IF ASKED "Why 11 and not 12 like your earlier run?": The first run used 1,000 random permutations and one borderline seed, MolTrans random seed 1, had a sampled p of 0.049. The exact p-value is 0.056, so that cell moves to agreeing and the count is 11. IF ASKED "How do you know the seeds were really different?": The per-seed scores differ for every model in the table. The MolTrans seed bug is how we know we would notice if they didn't. From now on, the cloud harness records a hash of the starting weights for every seed.

**Speaker notes:** SAY: To sum up. For the models we tested, a single attention figure can't support a claim that the model found the binding site. The verdict changes between seeds, it rarely survives a proper test, and it depends on choices papers don't report. What DOES hold up is coarser: three of the four models really do use what they attend to, and two focus on the kinase pocket at most levels. And as Figure 4 shows, a more accurate model is not a better localiser. Our message to the field is simple: use several seeds, compare with chance, correct your tests, state your readout, and check your splits by sequence. Two things sit beside the results. The benchmark itself leaks: 12 of the 88 "unseen" proteins were seen in training by sequence, and for the baseline we retrained the sequence match inflates AUROC on those rows by 0.116 (p = 0.0013), with no detectable inflation on the truly unseen rows. And the fix is the eight-point checklist on slide 8: each item exists because skipping it changed a result in our data. Every number in this paper is automatically traced back to the file it came from. IF ASKED "Isn't this just a negative result?": It is a negative result with a positive tool. The checklist and the released code let anyone test their own model before publishing. IF ASKED "Does it apply beyond kinases?": We don't claim it does. Both datasets are kinase panels, and we scope every claim to kinases.
